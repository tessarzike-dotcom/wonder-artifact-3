01 · The big idea

People are more than the first thing we notice.

In R. J. Palacio’s Wonder, Auggie Pullman’s facial difference influences how some people react to him. But appearance does not tell the whole story of a person’s humor, interests, worries, courage, or relationships.

What a first impression can do

Fill in the blanks

When people have little information, they may rely on assumptions. Those assumptions can shape whether someone is included, avoided, or treated unfairly.

What perspective can do

Make the picture bigger

Different narrators reveal different feelings and motivations. Looking through more than one perspective helps readers question an easy explanation.

Essential question: How do our perceptions of others influence the way we treat them?

Explore the idea

Choose a statement to reveal a thinking prompt.

Ask yourself: What can appearance tell us—and what can it never prove about a person’s character?

This feature paraphrases ideas and character situations from the novel; it does not replace close reading of the book.

Compare without generalizing

1. What does the person say about their own experience? Record a detail or short quotation and the source name.

2. What connects to Wonder? Identify a theme or situation that is similar, while noting that a real person’s life is not the same as a fictional character’s story.

3. What complicates the AI draft? Identify a detail about interests, goals, relationships, or individuality that the AI sentence leaves out.

4. What can you responsibly conclude? Write “This person’s account shows…” rather than “Everyone with a facial difference…”

Respectful reading reminder: focus on the person’s own words, agency, interests, and ideas—not on guessing medical details or treating appearance as the whole story.

02 · Switch perspectives

One story, more than one point of view

Select a perspective to explore how the same situation can carry different feelings and information. These are instructional paraphrases, not quotations from the novel.

Auggie’s perspective

Entering a new school can mean wondering whether others will see his interests and personality—or focus only on his appearance. He wants to be treated as a whole person.

Notice: His perspective centers the emotional impact of other people’s reactions.

A classmate’s perspective

A classmate may feel curious, uncertain, or influenced by what peers say. That uncertainty can lead to an unkind choice—or to reflection and a change in behavior.

Notice: A narrator’s limited knowledge can shape the tone and what the reader understands.

A family member’s perspective

A family member may want Auggie to feel safe and accepted while also knowing he needs room to make friendships and decisions himself.

Notice: Care and concern can coexist with a different view of what support should look like.

Click-to-think: Which perspective might reveal information the others do not know? What would you need to read in the novel before making a claim?
03 · AI literacy lab

Can you trust an AI-generated interpretation?

An AI tool can produce a confident-sounding description that is incomplete or too general. Your job is to check it against the novel and decide what deserves to stay.

AI-generated draft model · created for this learning activity

“Auggie’s experience is mainly about being different. Everyone notices his appearance, and he just wants people to be nice to him.”

This draft captures part of the topic, but it oversimplifies the character and makes a broad claim about “everyone.” It leaves out relationships, humor, interests, choices, and changes in other characters.

The model sentence is a newly generated instructional example, not a quotation from Wonder.

Make a decision

Choose Keep, Revise, or Reject. Then compare your reasoning with the feedback.

Your decision will appear here.

Try a stronger revision

One possible revision: Auggie’s experience includes the impact of others’ assumptions, but the novel also develops his personality and relationships through multiple perspectives. Readers should use specific scenes to show how belonging changes over time.

Before using this revision, locate scenes and evidence in your own copy of the novel. Do not treat AI output as proof.

04 · Evidence lab

Move from an idea to evidence

Tap each evidence target to reveal what to look for in the novel. Add your own page number, detail, or brief quotation from the edition you are reading.

Target A · First impressions
Look for a scene where a character reacts to Auggie’s appearance.
Record the scene and page number. What does the character assume? What does the text actually show? Explain how the reaction affects belonging.
Target B · A changing relationship
Look for a moment when a character’s understanding or behavior changes.
Record the action or dialogue that shows change. Does the character learn something new, reconsider a belief, or respond to peer pressure?
Target C · More than one perspective
Compare how two narrators or characters understand a situation.
Write one detail from each perspective. Explain what the second view adds, complicates, or corrects about the first.

06 · Instructor / course connections

How this artifact connects to the course framework

This interactive model supports the Grade 8 unit “Identity, Belonging, and Perspective: Seeing Beyond Assumptions.” It is a mentor feature and AI-evaluation activity—not a substitute for students’ own reading, evidence, or writing.

SAMR

Best-supported level for this artifact: Modification. The task is redesigned as a connected inquiry: students move between the novel, linked first-person nonfiction accounts, a labeled AI-generated interpretation, and their own evidence notes. They compare representations, record source-based reasoning, and can listen to their own synthesis. This is more than a digital worksheet, but the current individual activity can still be completed on paper, so I do not claim that the artifact itself reaches Redefinition.

Possible Redefinition extension: Students collaboratively create and publish an accessible digital feature that pairs their evidence-based analysis with approved links to first-person accounts, captions or audio, and an AI-transparency note. With permission, they could invite feedback from a school audience or a relevant community organization and revise their public-facing explanation in response. That authentic, shareable, multimodal exchange—not clicking alone—is the stronger Redefinition case.

ELA principle 1 · Complex text

Students return to Wonder to interpret characterization, dialogue, and differences among narrators. The “Switch perspectives” section frames a question, but the novel remains the source students must read closely to answer it.

ELA principle 2 · Use evidence

The evidence lab asks students to identify scenes, page numbers, and details, then explain how evidence supports a claim. The quiz contrasts specific, reasoned evidence with broad claims and unsupported AI output.

ELA principle 3 · Build background knowledge

The nonfiction text set adds first-person accounts from people with facial differences. Students build knowledge about varied lived experiences, then compare these accounts with the novel and AI draft. The prompts explicitly warn students not to treat one person’s story as representative of everyone.

Science of Reading (SOR)

The artifact supports comprehension and language through explicit vocabulary/concept framing, short chunks of text, repeated exposure to key ideas, retrieval through the quiz, and oral-language support through read-aloud. It aligns with the language-comprehension side of the Reading Rope—background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning, and literacy knowledge. It does not replace systematic decoding/word-reading instruction.

Layered multimodality

  • Visual: color-coded sections, symbolic “LOOK / LISTEN / LEARN” circles, cards, and visual hierarchy organize the central idea.
  • Auditory: browser text-to-speech reads the introduction or student notes aloud; students can also explore a linked video project where young people speak about their own interests and goals.
  • Interactivity: students navigate between literary and nonfiction sources, record a source note, evaluate an AI claim, reveal evidence prompts, and listen back to their own synthesis.

The modes contribute to one interpretation rather than operating as decoration: a student reads a first-person account, listens to youth describe themselves in their own voices, records a detail, and compares it with the AI draft and Wonder. Visual organization keeps the sources distinct; audio makes the speaker’s own voice available; the comparison prompt requires students to explain how the nonfiction detail complicates an assumption. This supports the unit’s movement from literary analysis to evidence-based, multimodal communication.

Use of generative AI

Tool: ChatGPT (OpenAI), used to draft the initial instructional concept and sample AI-generated interpretation for this artifact.

Prompt 1: “Using my Grade 8 ELA unit on Wonder, identity, belonging, and perspective, design a student-facing interactive mentor feature that helps students question first impressions and compare perspectives. Include clickable learning steps and an evidence-based writing task. Do not write students’ final response.”

Prompt 2: “Create a short, clearly labeled AI-generated draft interpretation of Auggie’s experience that is plausible but oversimplified, so Grade 8 students can evaluate it with Keep, Revise, or Reject decisions. Do not use direct quotations from the novel or present the AI draft as factual evidence.”

Prompt 3: “Explain how this interactive feature can demonstrate SAMR, the three ELA principles—complex text, using evidence, and building background knowledge—and Science of Reading language-comprehension strands. Identify visual, auditory, and interactive modes, and explain how they work together.”

Prompt 4: “Revise the Grade 8 Wonder artifact to include a short nonfiction text set with first-person accounts from people with facial differences. Use credible organizations’ own story pages or video projects, provide direct source links, and add prompts for comparing lived experience with the novel and an AI-generated claim. Explicitly remind students not to generalize from one person’s experience.”

Prompt 5: “Calibrate the SAMR explanation honestly. Identify the level the current artifact actually supports, distinguish it from an extension that could reach Redefinition, and explain the role of an authentic audience and student-created multimodal publication.”

Evaluation and refinement: I treated AI output as a starting point, not an authority. I edited the model to avoid invented quotations and unsupported specifics, labeled the sample as an AI-generated draft, required students to verify claims against their own copy of the novel, and added explicit evidence prompts. In response to instructor feedback, I added linked first-person nonfiction sources, comparison prompts that discourage generalization, and a more precise SAMR claim: Modification for the current activity, with Redefinition reserved for a student-created publication shared with an authentic audience. I also included accessible text, keyboard-operable buttons, audio controls, and a clear distinction between AI suggestions and student-authored analysis. Students—not AI—make and justify the final interpretive decisions.

Standards connection: NYS Grade 8 ELA standards in the unit plan include 8R1 (textual evidence), 8R3 (characterization/events), 8R6 (point of view and perspective), 8R7 (media), 8R9 (connections), and 8W1/8W5 (argument and revision).
