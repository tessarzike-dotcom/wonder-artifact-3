Beyond First Impressions

What changes when we pause, check the evidence, and try to see a person from more than one perspective?

Explore identity, belonging, and representation through Wonder by R. J. Palacio. Then investigate how an AI-generated interpretation can sound convincing while leaving out important complexity.

01 · Start here

One person. More than one story.

👀

First impressions are fast.

A person may notice appearance, a single action, or what other people say. That information can shape a first impression, but it cannot tell a whole life story.
🔎

Interpretations need evidence.

Readers can slow down, examine what the text actually shows, and revise a first idea when new details or another perspective changes the picture.

A strong interpretation does not just say what a character is like. It explains how the text leads us to that interpretation—and what we may still be missing.

Essential question

How do identity, assumptions, and perspective shape a character’s experience of belonging?

As you explore, keep the novel as your primary source. This feature does not replace reading the complex text; it gives you a structure for thinking about it.

02 · Build background knowledge

Vocabulary before you explore

Learn these words before analyzing Wonder and real-life stories. Knowing the words will help you explain ideas clearly and notice how people experience the world differently.

🪞

Identity

The qualities, experiences, relationships, and interests that help make someone who they are.

Example: Auggie’s identity includes much more than his appearance.

🤝

Belonging

The feeling of being accepted, included, and valued by a group.

Example: A student feels belonging when classmates make room for their ideas.

👀

Appearance

The way a person looks on the outside.

Example: Appearance alone does not tell us someone’s personality or story.

🪟

Perspective

The way a person sees, understands, or experiences a situation.

Example: Two characters may experience the same event differently.

⚠️

Assumption

A belief we accept without having enough evidence to know it is true.

Example: Assuming someone is unfriendly because they are quiet.

💛

Empathy

Trying to understand another person’s feelings or experiences.

Example: Listening to someone’s story instead of deciding how they must feel.

Read each example and choose the word that best matches it.

Choose an answer for each question, then check your understanding.

Reading connection: Explicitly learning key words, discussing examples, and using the words again during reading builds vocabulary and background knowledge. Return to these terms as you explore each activity.

03 · Interactive perspective switch

Switch the lens

The same event can be interpreted differently depending on who is looking, what they know, and what they may not understand yet. Select a lens, then compare what each lens notices.

Student’s lens

What might this person notice?

A student may notice who includes someone, who avoids eye contact, or how classmates respond. But a single moment cannot prove what everyone thinks or why they acted that way.

Check the text: Which action, dialogue, or reaction in Wonder supports your interpretation? What else could explain the moment?

Try it: what does this lens make easier to see?

05 · Close-reading evidence lab

Move from opinion to evidence

Return to the complex text, Wonder. Choose a specific moment and gather evidence before deciding what it means. Page numbers vary by edition, so use your own copy and record the chapter or page.

Evidence finder

1
Notice
Find an action, a line of dialogue, a reaction, or a narrator’s detail connected to identity or belonging.
2
Record
Write a short quotation or accurate paraphrase and note the page/chapter.
3
Explain
Explain how the detail supports your interpretation. Do not let the quotation speak for itself.
4
Complicate
Ask what another character might notice or what the evidence does not prove.

07 · Create, connect, and share

Final activity: Beyond First Impressions — My Multimodal Message

Now bring your learning together. Create a short message about a theme in Wonder—such as identity, belonging, empathy, appearance, or assumptions. Combine your own writing with a visual and/or audio recording. Use evidence from the novel and connect it to a real-life first-person story you explored.

Your mission

1
Choose a theme
Pick one idea you want your audience to understand.
2
Connect two sources
Use a specific moment from Wonder and a detail or idea from a real-life first-person account. Record the chapter/page and the nonfiction source title.
3
Create your message
Write a short explanation, then add a meaningful image, drawing, or audio narration. Each mode should help communicate your idea.
4
Reflect and share
Explain what you learned from the two sources and prepare your work for classmates or another teacher-approved audience.

Why create this?

You are not just answering questions about a text—you are combining literary evidence, a real person’s perspective, and your own interpretation into a new message for an audience. Thoughtful use of writing, visuals, and audio can make your meaning clearer and give readers more than one way to engage with your ideas.

Teaching connections: Vocabulary and background knowledge support the Science of Reading. The original, multimodal product can strengthen a SAMR Redefinition connection when students synthesize sources and share an accessible message with an authentic audience—not simply transfer a worksheet onto a screen.
